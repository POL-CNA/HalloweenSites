The Secretary at Parma Station

In the autumn of 1961, a few months after the last freight train rolled out of Parma, Ohio, two fifteen-year-old boys stood at the edge of the abandoned B&O station, straddling their bikes in the fading light.

"Weekend's the best time," John said, clicking his flashlight on and off. "Nobody around."

Eddy, round-faced and never quite as brave as he pretended, shrugged. "Nobody alive around, you mean."

"Don't be a baby."

They dropped their bikes in the weeds and crept toward the two-story building. Its windows were dark and its paint hung in curls. The office door was locked, but a big garage door sat partly open, just wide enough for two skinny boys to squeeze through.

Inside, the warehouse swallowed their flashlight beams. Four doors lined one wall, and the hallway beyond them smelled of mildew and decades of dust. They chose the dispatch office at the end.

It was exactly what they'd hoped for. Rows of old metal desks stood against the walls, crowded with dead typewriters and ancient machines. A huge, yellowed map covered the far wall, its rail lines fanning out like veins. The drawers they tugged open held only moldy folders and silence.

Then the silence broke.

Click. Click. Click. Click.

Both boys froze.

"You hear that?" Eddy whispered.

Click. Click. Click.

It was steady and patient, the sound of someone working. They swept their lights across the room, but there was no one there. The building had been empty for months.

"John." Eddy's voice cracked. "Over here. Now."

On a large desk in the center of the room sat an old adding machine, all keys and a crank arm on the side. As their beams settled on it, one key sank down by itself. Then another. Then another.

Click. Click. Click.

Nobody was touching it. Nobody was anywhere near it.

John felt his mouth go dry. He couldn't look away. It was as though the machine were tallying something, and he didn't want to know what.

Eddy grabbed his sleeve. "We're leaving. We're leaving right now." He shoved John toward the door, and John, who had never once seen his portly friend run, discovered Eddy could move like a jackrabbit. They burst through the warehouse and out into the cool night, and didn't stop until they reached their bikes.

Eddy doubled over, wheezing, his face the color of candle wax.

"What happened?" John panted. "What's wrong with you?"

"She was right there," Eddy whispered. "In my ear. A woman. She said..." He swallowed. "She said, Stop poking around, and get out."

"Eddy, there was nobody in there but us."

"I know what I heard."

John opened his mouth to argue. Then something made him glance back at the building.

"Eddy," he said quietly. "Look."

In one of the office windows, pale against the black, a face was watching them.

It was an older woman, her gray hair set in tight, short curls, her dress cut in the style of the 1940s. She held a clipboard pressed against her chest like a shield. She wasn't sad or lost. Her expression was cold and stern, the look of someone whose workplace had been invaded by two careless children.

Neither boy said a word. They scrambled onto their bikes and pedaled home as fast as their legs would carry them, never once looking back.

The next day, John found Eddy sitting on his porch steps, hollow-eyed.

"I never believed in ghosts," Eddy said. "Not really."

"Me neither," said John. Then, after a pause: "I think she was a secretary. Maybe she worked there for years. Maybe that desk was the only place she ever felt important." He shook his head slowly. "We trespassed in her office. I think she was just letting us know."

"Letting us know," Eddy repeated, with a shaky laugh. "She chased us out."

They never spoke of it to anyone else, and they never went back.

The following year, the wrecking crews came and tore the old station to the ground, board by board, until nothing was left but dirt and weeds. John and Eddy wondered, from time to time, what became of the woman with the clipboard once her desk was gone.

Had she finally moved on?

Or was she still out there somewhere in Parma, waiting for a new office, and the quiet click, click, click of her work to begin again?
