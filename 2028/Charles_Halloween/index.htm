⫘⫘⫘The Soul Keeper⫘⫘⫘

Enter the tunnel if you dare

A ghost story from Franklin Avenue, Cleveland

In 1941, a “social club” for German-Americans paid cash for the old Tiedemann house. The neighbors said spies. For once, they were close.

Captain Karl Weber ran the cell from the cellar, where Hans Tiedemann’s forgotten tunnels hid radios, forged papers, and a map of the world stuck with red pins. He had a limp, a leather folder, and a belief that an empire could be taken one quiet house at a time. Cleveland was only the first cell in a network that stretched from Ohio to Buenos Aires, waiting on a word from Berlin.

He never asked why a rich man had dug the tunnels. That was his first mistake.

The trouble was the silence. Every transmission came back with something underneath it: a soft, patient sound, like someone breathing in.

Then young Fritz, the radioman, went white at his desk.

“Sleep, little child, sleep,” he whispered. “My mother sang that. How does it know it?”

Weber told him to stop talking nonsense. That night he stood at the cellar door and listened, and he knew the feeling that climbed the tunnel toward him. He had spent his life teaching it to other men.

Otto, a gray old clerk from the Rhine valley, finally came to him.

“Back home we had a story about a soul keeper. A dead thing that cannot rest, so it collects the living. It starts with a family and takes one soul at a time, and it keeps them. The Tiedemanns died here, little Emma first. It has six of them. It wants the seventh.”

“And if the family is gone?”

“Then it will take anyone close enough to the house.”

Men began to go quiet, one a night. They walked through the cellar like sleepers, their eyes open and nobody behind them, and by morning they were simply gone. Fritz first, his headphones left on the floor, still warm, still playing the lullaby. Then two couriers. Then the man who kept the ledgers. Otto sewed a coin into every collar, as his grandmother had taught him, to pay for a way out. It did not help.

Weber burned the papers. He flooded the tunnels. He cemented shut the narrow door behind the library shelves. The voice followed him from room to room, and each time it counted, it counted a little more correctly.

One for Emma. Two for Grandma Greta.

Three, four, five, six...

and one more to keep.

On the last night he stood in the great hall beneath the carved motto, My house is my fortress, and for the first time in his life he had no orders to give.

“Seven,” said the voice, very close now, almost kind.

“I am not a Tiedemann,” Weber whispered.

“No,” said the voice. “But you are in the house.”

REPORT, FEDERAL BUREAU OF INVESTIGATION, CLEVELAND FIELD OFFICE. Premises entered April 1942. Located: one shortwave set, one world map with pins, forged documents. Personnel: none. Eleven overcoats found hanging on hooks in the cellar, each with a coin sewn into the collar. Case closed.

The agents noticed one more thing and left it out of the report. When they climbed to the tower and looked out over the street, every window of the house held a tiny candle flame, and none of them flickered.

They counted eleven lights. Then they counted the lights behind those, and stopped.
